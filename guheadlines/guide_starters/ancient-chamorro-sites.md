People sailed to the Mariana Islands about 3,500 years ago, one of the earliest long ocean crossings anywhere in the world. Their descendants, the CHamoru people, built villages along the coasts, fished the reefs and farmed the land. Around a thousand years ago they began to raise their houses on latte, and the stones are still found all over the island.

## What latte are

A latte is a stone pillar in two parts: a column, the haligi, topped with a rounded cap stone, the tasa, set with its flat side up. Latte stood in two parallel rows and held up the floor of a house, a canoe shed or a meeting house. Most are a few feet tall; the largest ever cut still lie in an ancient quarry on the neighboring island of Rota, never finished.

Latte were still in use when Spanish missionaries arrived in the 1600s. Today the latte is one of the island's best known symbols, on buildings, signs and jewelry.

## Latte Stone Park, Hagåtña

The easiest latte to see stand in a small park near the plaza in Hagåtña. They were moved here from an old village site in the south. See [A morning in Hagåtña](/guides/hagatna-walking).

## The Guam Museum

Also in Hagåtña, the Guam Museum tells the story of the CHamoru people from the first settlers to today, with tools, pottery, shell ornaments and other objects found on the island. Check the opening days and times before you go.

## Pagat

On the east coast, a steep trail leads down through limestone forest to the remains of Pagat, an ancient village. Latte, some fallen, and lusong, stone mortars used for pounding food, still lie among the trees. The trail continues to a sea cave with a freshwater pool. See [Hiking and waterfalls](/guides/hiking-and-waterfalls).

## Ritidian

The wildlife refuge at Guam's northern tip protects latte sites and caves with ancient rock paintings, along with its beach and forest. Ask at the refuge's visitor center about the cultural sites. The refuge is open only some days of the week; see [A day in northern Guam](/guides/northern-guam).

## Gef Pa'go, Inarajan

In the village of Inarajan in the south, Gef Pa'go is a cultural village where elders show visitors traditional skills: weaving with coconut and pandanus leaves, cooking and other crafts. It is usually open in the mornings. Combine it with the Inarajan pools and the rest of [Driving the southern villages](/guides/south-loop-drive).

## Valley of the Latte, Talofofo

In the Talofofo River valley, boat and kayak tours wind up the river through the jungle, with stops to see latte and demonstrations of traditional crafts.

## Living culture

CHamoru culture is not only in the past. You can hear chants, see dances by local cultural groups and watch weavers and carvers at work at the Wednesday night market in Hagåtña, at festivals, and especially in March, CHamoru Month. See [Fiestas and festivals through the year](/guides/festivals-and-fiestas).

## Visit with respect

- **Never sit on, climb or move latte,** and don't lean on them for photos.
- **Take nothing:** stones, pottery, shells and bones at old sites are protected by law, and taking them is deeply disrespectful.
- **Stay on the path** at village sites, and keep your voice down.
- Many people on Guam ask the taotaomo'na, the spirits of the ancient CHamoru people, for permission before entering the jungle around old sites. Read [Local customs and being a good guest](/guides/culture-and-respect).
