Typhoons are part of life on Guam. Most years bring a few storms that pass close by, and every few years one hits the island hard. People who have lived here a long time take them seriously, prepare early and help each other afterward. If you are new to Guam, learn the basics in your first weeks, before a storm is on the way, and build your supplies a little at a time.

## When typhoons come

Storms can form in any month in the western Pacific, but the season is busiest from about July to November. A typhoon can strengthen quickly, so a storm that looks far away can be at the island in a few days. Follow the forecasts from the National Weather Service office on Guam, and the [weather page](/weather) on this site, which shows warnings as soon as they are issued.

## The readiness levels

The government and the military use Conditions of Readiness, counting down as a storm comes closer:

- **Condition 4:** damaging winds are possible within 72 hours. This is the normal level all through typhoon season: keep your supplies stocked.
- **Condition 3:** damaging winds are possible within 48 hours. Check your supplies, fill the car with gas, get cash and make your plan.
- **Condition 2:** damaging winds are expected within 24 hours. Put up shutters, bring everything in from outside, and finish your preparations.
- **Condition 1:** damaging winds are expected within 12 hours. Stay inside; only emergency workers should be on the roads.

The military may add its own instructions for bases and families. Follow the announcements from your base and the Government of Guam.

## Supplies to keep at home

Build a kit for at least a week without power or water, and keep it all season:

- **Water:** at least a gallon a person a day, plus water for washing and for pets. Fill bathtubs and containers before the storm.
- **Food** that needs no cooking or fridge, and a manual can opener.
- **Light:** flashlights and lanterns with spare batteries; avoid candles.
- **Power:** charged power banks, and a generator with fuel if you have one, used only outdoors, far from windows.
- **Medicines** for two weeks, and a first-aid kit.
- **Cash:** card machines and ATMs stop when the power goes out.
- **A battery radio** for news when phones and the internet are down.
- **Important papers** in a waterproof bag: IDs, orders, insurance, medical records.
- **Supplies for babies and pets.**
- **Tools:** tarps, rope, duct tape, a saw and work gloves for the clean-up.

## Getting the house ready

- **Shutters:** know where they are and how they go up, and practice before you need to. Ask your landlord or housing office who puts them up.
- **Outside:** bring in or tie down anything the wind can throw: chairs, grills, bins, plants and toys.
- **Trees:** trim branches near the house early in the season, not when a storm is coming.
- **Inside:** choose the safest room, away from windows, and keep it clear.
- **Cars:** park away from trees and poles, with a full tank.
- **Fridge:** turn it to the coldest setting, and freeze bottles of water to keep food cold longer.
- **Photos:** take pictures of your home and belongings before the storm, for insurance.

## During the storm

Stay inside, away from windows and doors, until officials say it is safe. The calm when the eye passes over can last a while, and the winds come back from the other side. Don't go out to look.

## After the storm

- Wait for the all-clear before going out. Stay away from fallen power lines, and treat every line as live.
- Power and water can be off for days or longer after a strong storm. Use your stored water, and boil or treat tap water until officials say it is safe.
- Check on your neighbors, especially older people and families with small children.
- Take photos of damage before you clean up, and report it to your landlord or housing office and your insurer.
- Run generators only outdoors, far from doors and windows: their fumes kill.

## Learn from your neighbors

Ask your sponsor, coworkers and neighbors about the storms they have lived through. Guam's people have come through many typhoons, and the best advice is local.
