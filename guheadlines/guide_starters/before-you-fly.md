A little preparation makes the first days on Guam much easier. Guam is part of the United States, but it is a long way from everywhere, it has its own customs rules, and it is hot and wet all year. Here is what to sort out before you fly.

## Entry papers

- **US citizens:** bring a passport. Many flights to Guam connect through Japan, Korea or the Philippines, where you need one, and airlines may ask for it.
- **Visitors from other countries:** some can visit Guam without a visa, under a program for Guam and the Northern Mariana Islands or with an approved travel authorization. The rules depend on your passport and change from time to time, so check them with US Customs and Border Protection, or the US embassy in your country, before you book.
- Make sure your passport is valid for your whole stay, and longer if you travel on.

## The customs form

Guam has its own customs and quarantine agency, separate from the mainland. Every arriving traveler or family fills in a customs declaration, on paper or online. Fresh fruit, plants, soil, meat and some other foods are restricted, to keep pests and diseases away from the island. When in doubt, declare it. Pets have their own rules.

## Money

- Guam uses the US dollar.
- Cards are taken in hotels, restaurants, malls and most shops, and there are ATMs across the island.
- Carry some cash for markets, fruit stands, fiestas and small local restaurants.
- Tipping is as on the US mainland: about 15 to 20 percent in restaurants, and a little for drivers, guides and hotel staff.
- Guam has no general sales tax.

## Phones and the internet

US mobile plans often work on Guam, but some charge it as roaming: check with yours before you leave. Visitors from other countries can buy a local SIM or eSIM at the airport or in town. Hotels, cafés and malls have Wi-Fi. Guam's time zone is ten hours ahead of UTC, with no daylight saving time.

## Power plugs

Guam uses the same plugs and voltage as the US mainland (110 to 120 volts, flat two-pin and three-pin plugs). Visitors from Asia, Australia and Europe need an adapter, and a converter for devices that only take 220 to 240 volts. Most phone and laptop chargers take both.

## What to pack

- **Light clothes** that dry quickly, and one long-sleeve layer for cold air conditioning and planes.
- **A rain jacket or small umbrella:** short showers come and go, especially from July to November.
- **Sun protection:** a hat, sunglasses and plenty of sunscreen. Choose a reef-safe sunscreen to protect the coral.
- **Water shoes or reef shoes** for rocky beaches and the reef flat.
- **Snorkel gear** if you have your own; you can also rent it.
- **Good shoes** for trails, which are often muddy and slippery.
- **A reusable water bottle.** Drink much more water than you think you need.

## Health and safety

- The sun and the heat are the most common problems: plan the middle of the day indoors or in the shade.
- Read the rules about the sea before you swim. Currents near reef openings are strong, and some beaches are dangerous for swimmers. See [Beaches and staying safe in the water](/guides/beaches-and-water-safety).
- Bring the medicines you need, with their names written down. Pharmacies on Guam sell the usual US brands.
- Travel insurance with medical cover is a good idea, especially for diving.

## When to go

The dry season, from about December to June, has less rain and calmer weather. July to November is wetter, and typhoons are most likely then. See [Weather and the best time to visit](/guides/weather-and-when-to-visit), and check the [weather page](/weather) in the days before you fly.
