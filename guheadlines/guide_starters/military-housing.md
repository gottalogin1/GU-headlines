Housing is the biggest question for most families moving to Guam. You can live on base, in housing run for the military, or off base, renting a house or apartment in one of the villages with a housing allowance. Both work well. The right choice depends on the wait lists, your family, and where you will work. This guide explains the choices and, above all, what to check in a home on an island of heat, rain and typhoons.

## Start with the housing office

Contact the military housing office for your base as soon as you have orders, and visit it in your first days on Guam. It runs the wait lists for base housing and, for those who live off base, keeps lists of homes and checks leases. Many services require members to see the housing office before signing a lease off base: ask before you sign anything.

## Living on base

**Good:** no commute to work, homes built for typhoons with the upkeep done for you, playgrounds and neighbors in the same situation, and schools on or near base.

**Less good:** wait lists, especially in the busy summer moving season; less choice of size and place; and the base rules for pets, guests and changes to the home.

## Living off base

Members who live off base receive an overseas housing allowance for rent, with help for utilities and moving in. The amounts depend on rank, family and the rules of the year, and they change: the finance and housing offices have the current figures.

**Good:** more choice, a chance to live by the sea or in a quiet village, and a closer feel for island life.

**Less good:** a commute; landlords and leases that vary; and costs that come with the house, such as power, water and upkeep.

## Where to look

Traffic on Guam is light by mainland standards, but the main roads get busy at rush hour. Many families choose a village near the base where they work:

- **Near Andersen Air Force Base and Camp Blaz, in the north:** Yigo and Dededo, with homes from new developments to quiet country lots.
- **Near Naval Base Guam, in the southwest:** Santa Rita, Agat and Piti, with sea views and a quieter pace.
- **In the middle of the island:** Tamuning, Barrigada, Mangilao and the villages around Hagåtña, close to shops, restaurants and the naval hospital, with commutes north and south.

Drive the villages at different times of day before you choose: rush-hour traffic, the noise of dogs and roosters, and how far the shops are all matter.

## What to check in a home

Guam's weather is hard on houses. Look closely:

- **Built of concrete:** concrete homes stand up to typhoons far better than wood and metal ones. Ask what the house is made of, and how it did in the last big typhoons.
- **Typhoon shutters** for every window and glass door, and who puts them up.
- **A generator, or a hook-up for one,** for the power cuts after storms.
- **A water tank:** some homes have one for water cuts.
- **Mold and leaks:** look and smell in closets, under sinks and on ceilings. Ask how often the home has been treated.
- **Air conditioning:** which rooms have it, how old it is, and who services it. Power is expensive on Guam, so ask what the bills have been.
- **Termites and pests:** ask when the home was last treated.
- **Water pressure and hot water,** and the state of the roof and gutters.
- **Parking and security:** a covered carport protects the car from sun and salt.

## The lease

- Read it all, and make sure the military clause lets you end it early if you get new orders.
- Write down who pays for what: power, water, internet, trash, lawn care and pest control.
- Agree on who handles typhoon shutters and repairs after a storm.
- Walk through the home with the landlord before you move in, and take photos and video of everything.
- Pay the deposit by a method that leaves a record.

## Working with a local agent

A licensed local real estate agent who knows the military clause, the allowances and the villages can save you time, especially while you are still in temporary lodging. Ask the housing office and your sponsor for names, and ask any agent which homes they have shown to military families before.

## While you wait

Families usually stay in a hotel or in base lodging for the first weeks. Use that time to drive the villages, talk to your sponsor and coworkers about where they live, and read [Getting your home ready for typhoon season](/guides/typhoon-ready-home).
